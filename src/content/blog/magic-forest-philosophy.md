Is mathematics created or discovered? Do we invent its objects and structures, or do they exist independently of us, waiting to be found? My work on *The Magic Forest* brought this question into my artistic practice.

In my [previous article](https://doritgenart.netlify.app/blog/magic-forest-tech/), I described the Magic Forest project and the mathematical processes behind its images. The unexpected results made me wonder what I had created and what, if anything, I had discovered. These questions led me to explore Plato’s philosophy and mathematical Platonism.

I begin with Plato’s theory of Forms, his distinction between sensible and intelligible reality, and the place of mathematics within it. I then turn to modern mathematical Platonism and some of the mathematicians who have shaped it, before returning to the Magic Forest to consider how these ideas might illuminate my work.

# Plato — The Reality of the Intelligible

For Plato, reality extends beyond what our senses perceive. Sensible things change, decay and disappear; the Forms, grasped through thought, are stable and independent of their particular manifestations. A beautiful object may lose its beauty, but Beauty itself does not change. Plato therefore attributes a more fundamental reality to the intelligible. In the *Republic*, three connected analogies, the Sun, the Divided Line and the Cave, develop this relationship between what is real and how we know it.

**The Sun analogy** explains what makes understanding possible. Just as sunlight enables the eye to see sensible objects, the **Good** enables the mind to know intelligible ones. And as the Sun supports generation and growth, the Good gives intelligible things not only their knowability but also their very being.

**The Divided Line** distinguishes four levels: images, sensible things, mathematical reasoning and dialectical understanding. Mathematics moves thought into the intelligible realm. A geometer may draw a triangle, but the proof concerns the triangle is understood through thought, not the imperfect drawing. Yet mathematical reasoning starts from hypotheses it does not justify; dialectic goes further, questioning those hypotheses and seeking the principles on which they rest. Mathematics thus occupies an intermediate position in Plato’s account of knowledge, though whether its objects are Forms or a distinct intermediate kind is not clearly settled.

**The Cave** gives this movement a human dimension. Prisoners who have seen only shadows take them for reality. A released prisoner gradually discovers what produces the shadows and finally emerges into sunlight. Education, for Plato, is this reorientation of the mind toward what explains appearances. Mathematics helps accomplish it by directing attention beyond visible things toward intelligible objects and relations, preparing the mind for dialectic.

For my work, this raises a question about the relationship between the visible image and the mathematics that makes it possible. Plato offers a framework in which mathematical objects and relations have a reality independent of their visual manifestations. But that does not yet establish that the creatures I recognize in the Magic Forest exist independently of the image, or of my perception of it.

# Plato’s Modern Heirs: Mathematical Platonism

Mathematical Platonism holds that mathematical objects, such as numbers, sets, functions and geometric figures, really exist, independently of human minds, language and practice. They are abstract: they occupy no place, have no age and cause nothing. In this view, the mathematician is an explorer rather than an inventor. When Euclid proved that there are infinitely many primes, he discovered a truth that held before anyone thought of it and would hold if no one ever had.

Platonism appeals to mathematicians because it matches the experience of doing mathematics. Problems resist our wishes, results surprise even those who find them, and thinkers across cultures and centuries keep arriving at the same objects and hidden connections, as if exploring a landscape that already exists. Fermat’s Last Theorem remained unproven for over 350 years until Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994, yet it seems natural to say it was true all along. Platonism also helps explain why mathematics works so well in the real world: curved geometries studied in the 1800s as pure abstraction later turned out to describe Einstein’s spacetime, and today they keep GPS accurate. Much of this appeal is psychological rather than argued, and critics note that a rich enough set of invented rules can surprise its own creators too. Gödel thought the feeling was a genuine form of perception; skeptics think it is a useful illusion. That disagreement is, in a sense, the whole debate in miniature.

The intuition is ancient, but the modern position took shape in the late 19th century with **Gottlob Frege**. In *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), Frege argued that numbers are objects, not ideas in our heads. If numbers were mental, each person would have their own “two,” and arithmetic would become psychology. Since arithmetic is objective and shared, its subject matter must be objective too. In 1934, **Paul Bernays** popularized the name “Platonism” for this stance.

The name points back to Plato: like his Forms, mathematical objects are taken to be eternal, unchanging and known through reason rather than the senses. Modern Platonists keep this core but usually leave out the rest of Plato’s metaphysics, such as the Form of the Good and the immortal soul. Modern Platonism is a thesis about what exists, not a complete philosophical system.

**Kurt Gödel** (1906–1978) was its most famous twentieth-century champion. He claimed we have a kind of perception of sets, since the axioms “force themselves upon us as being true,” and he believed that open questions such as the Continuum Hypothesis have definite answers.

Philosophers have raised serious objections. The sharpest is **epistemological**, set out by Paul Benacerraf in “Mathematical Truth” (1973) and later sharpened by Hartry Field. If numbers exist outside space and time and cause nothing, how can we know anything about them? Our knowledge of tables and stars depends on causal contact, but abstract objects offer no such channel, so Platonism seems unable to explain how mathematical knowledge is possible.

**W.V.O. Quine** (1908–2000) and **Hilary Putnam** (1926–2016) replied that we know mathematical objects the way we know electrons: indirectly, because they are indispensable to our best-confirmed scientific theories. Accepting physics while rejecting numbers is, in Putnam’s phrase, “intellectual dishonesty.” Other Platonists reject the **causal theory of knowledge** itself, arguing that mathematics is known *a priori*, through proof and reflection, and that Benacerraf’s challenge rests on a theory of knowledge built for physical objects.

Other schools add further objections: **nominalists** find a realm of abstract objects extravagant and argue that science can do without numbers, while **fictionalists** treat mathematics as a useful story.

# Roger Penrose: Three Worlds and a Forest

Today’s most prominent Platonist is probably **Sir Roger Penrose**, the Oxford mathematical physicist who won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on black holes. In *The Road to Reality* (2004) he describes three worlds, the physical, the mental and the Platonic world of mathematical truths, each arising from another in a mysterious loop. A small part of mathematics governs the whole physical world, a small part of the physical world (our brains) gives rise to minds, and minds grasp a small part of mathematics.

His favorite example of discovery is the **Mandelbrot set**, a fractal produced by a remarkably simple formula. When it was first plotted by computer, no one expected its endless, intricate detail. Penrose argues that nobody designed this complexity; like Mount Everest, it was simply there to be found. This is my Magic Forest question on a grander scale: I specified functions and rules, and creatures appeared.

Penrose goes further. In *The Emperor’s New Mind* (1989) he draws on Gödel’s incompleteness theorems to argue that human mathematicians can see truths that no fixed computational system can prove, so human understanding cannot be purely algorithmic. This bears directly on my forest: the patterns were produced by an algorithm, but the *seeing* of creatures in them was mine, the very part Penrose believes no algorithm captures.

His argument is far from accepted. Critics note that recognizing the truth of Gödel’s unprovable statement requires knowing that the system is consistent, and no mathematician can be certain their own reasoning is free of error; we too may be fallible machines. Today’s AI systems, which now solve demanding mathematical problems, make the question more relevant than ever.

# Back to the Magic Forest

So, did I create the creatures of the Magic Forest, or were they waiting for me?

Penrose’s three worlds offer a way to answer, because the Magic Forest touches all three. In the Platonic world are the noise functions, vector fields and transformations; once their parameters are fixed, every trajectory is determined, whether or not anyone ever computes it. In the physical world are the pixels, thousands of colored lines on a black screen, like shadows on the wall of Plato’s cave. And in the mental world is the moment I looked at a tangle of lines and saw a creature.

Seen this way, the answer is both. The shapes were discovered: like the Mandelbrot set, they were implicit in the mathematics before I rendered them, and I could not have designed them if I tried. But the forest was created. I chose which regions of this vast space to explore, which parameter ranges to stay within and which colors to light them with. And the creatures themselves, a beak here, a tendril or a wing there, exist only for an eye that recognizes them. Another viewer might walk the same paths and meet different creatures.

Perhaps this is what it means to make generative art: not to invent from nothing, nor to copy what is already there, but to wander through a landscape that exists independently of us and choose where to look. The mathematics holds the forest. The seeing makes it magic.
